Designed to the specification

Five pieces drawn at true proportion, from the same tokens as the site. Every measurement inside an artifact is in real millimetres, and the render is scaled by one number, so what you see is dimensionally correct. You can hand it to a designer as an intended layout, not a mood board.

Business card

90 × 54 mm, the Hong Kong standard. The face stays typographic. The reverse carries a cell field generated from the holder's own name, so every card in a department is recognisably the same system and no two are the same picture.

Hash · lead
Hash · accent

Click any line on the face to edit it. The field on the reverse regenerates from the name as you type, and Auto returns the colour the name hashed to. Lead is the family that carries the field, accent the one in about one cell in eight. Both move every register at once, and each renders the chosen slot in its own ink. Setting accent to match lead gives a single-family field.

Face · endorsed lockup in Gold

Reverse · Gold, Antique, Champagne, Gold Ink

Gold is a finish before it is a colour. Flat gold ink on a dark stock reads as mustard, and neither foil nor metallic Pantone can carry a gradient at all. Run this register as hot foil or PMS 871/872 C and let the cells go flat, or run it in process and accept that the screen preview above is optimistic. §07 gives the three plates the ramp needs if it has to print conventionally.

Type never sits on the family field. An earlier draft flooded the reverse with Ocean and set the lockup in ink on top of it, which gave 2.4:1 and looked like a mistake. None of the four families carries text at either polarity: white fails on Ocean at 3.7:1, and ink fails on Red. So the field and the type get their own zones, and the type keeps the full 16.9:1 it has on the stock. Same conclusion as the --on-fam rule in §07, reached the hard way.

The field is generated, not chosen. The holder's name goes through cyrb128 to 128 bits, and an sfc32 stream lays out each cell in order: quarter 50%, split square 16%, circle 12%, square 10%, empty 12%. One family leads, a second appears in about one cell in eight. The same name always returns the same field, so a card can be reprinted years later without keeping the file. It is the Lab's Your Mark generator, so a mark made there and a mark printed here are the same artwork.

Overriding the colour keeps the layout. Picking a family replaces the lead the hash chose and nothing else. The arrangement of cells stays keyed to the name, and the accent shifts only far enough not to collide with the new lead. Use it when a card has to sit inside a cohort or a Faculty that already owns a colour. Left on Auto, a department's cards spread across all four families on their own.

ID card

85.6 × 54 mm, the ISO/IEC 7810 ID-1 format every access system expects. The University card design governs; BDSIS contributes the mark, the scheme line, and a 6 mm column down the left edge generated from the holder's name. On a card whose whole job is identity, the stripe may as well be the student's own.

The rounded corner here is not ours. ID-1 mandates a 3.18 mm radius on all four corners so the card feeds through readers. It is the only place in the system where four rounded corners are correct, and it is a manufacturing constraint, not a design choice. Do not read it as licence elsewhere.

A panel is not a page. Everything on a board is sized from the distance it is read at, so the body copy here is 30 mm and would be absurd on a letterhead. The numbers in §06 are a screen scale and do not transfer. What does transfer is the hierarchy: one headline, one idea per panel, and nothing set below the size at which a passer-by can read it while walking.

Taking these to the PSD mockups

Each piece is drawn from the same tokens as the site, so the values are already correct: the golds and family colours in §07, the type scale in §06, the 8 px spacing base reinterpreted as millimetres. When you place these into the mockup PSDs, convert to CMYK with the printer's own profile, not the figures in the guidelines. Those are a first-proof conversion, not a measurement.
